Gravity at Scale: From Dark Energy to Attribution

What testing modified gravity theories taught me about building measurement frameworks — and why the hardest part is always deciding what counts as a test.

General relativity has passed every precision test to which it has been subjected. But nearly all of those tests were conducted in the strong-field, small-scale regime: the precession of Mercury's perihelion, the bending of light around the sun, the decay of binary pulsars. The behavior of gravity at cosmological scales — across hundreds of megaparsecs, in the presence of dark energy, where the equations are pushed into a regime they were never tested in — is much less constrained. My dissertation asked what galaxy surveys tell us about gravity at those scales, and how to extract that signal cleanly from everything else.

What It Means to Test a Theory

A test of gravity is a comparison between a theoretical prediction and an observation. The complication is that the prediction is not just a function of the gravitational theory. It depends on a long chain of other assumptions: the initial conditions of the universe, the physics of baryons, the selection function of the survey, the noise properties of the instrument. Isolating the gravitational signal means understanding all the other contributions well enough to separate them from it. This is a harder problem than it sounds, because most of those contributions are not known from first principles — they have to be modeled, and the models have their own parameters, and those parameters have to be constrained simultaneously with the ones you care about.

The result is that a test of gravity is really a test of a joint model — gravity plus everything else. A discrepancy between prediction and observation could mean the gravitational theory is wrong, or it could mean one of the auxiliary assumptions is wrong. Distinguishing these possibilities requires designing the analysis so that the gravitational signal and the astrophysical systematics have different signatures: they respond differently to scale, to redshift, to the galaxy population used as a tracer. When they do, you can separate them. When they don't, you can't, and claiming a test of gravity is claiming more than the data support.

The Measurement Problem in Practice

You don't observe gravity directly. You observe galaxy positions, shapes, and redshifts — each of which is a compressed, noisy, selection-biased projection of the underlying physics. Galaxy positions are distorted by peculiar velocities. Galaxy shapes are sheared by lensing and contaminated by intrinsic alignments. Redshifts measured photometrically have uncertainties that smear structure along the line of sight. Building a framework that propagates from observations to gravitational constraints without introducing uncontrolled systematics is the bulk of the technical work.

The approach that emerged from my dissertation was to work at the level of two-point statistics — the correlation functions and power spectra that summarize the clustering of galaxies and the shearing of background images — and to model each systematic effect explicitly rather than excising it. Excision discards information; explicit modeling uses it. The cost is a more complex likelihood with more parameters. The benefit is that the parameters encoding the systematics can themselves be constrained by the data, and the constraints on the gravitational parameters are automatically marginalized over the uncertainty in the systematics.

From Cosmology to Attribution

The skills transfer almost directly. In advertising measurement, you also don't observe causal effects directly. You observe clicks, impressions, and purchases — each a compressed, noisy, selection-biased projection of underlying consumer behavior. The challenge is the same: build a framework that extracts a causal signal from observational data without making assumptions you can't defend.

The analogy runs deeper than methodology. The core epistemic problem in both fields is the same: you are trying to infer the properties of a system you cannot directly manipulate from data generated by a process you do not fully control. In cosmology, you cannot run experiments on the universe. In advertising, you often cannot run experiments on the market. In both cases, the response is to be precise about what your identification strategy is, what assumptions it rests on, and what evidence would cause you to revise them. The vocabulary is different. The epistemology is identical.

What cosmology adds, beyond methodology, is a certain comfort with large uncertainty. Constraints on modified gravity are typically weak — you rule out large departures from general relativity, but the allowed space of theories remains large. The appropriate response is not to overstate what the data show but to report the constraint accurately and design the next experiment to tighten it. This disposition — measure carefully, report honestly, design the next test — is as useful in adtech as it is in physics.
