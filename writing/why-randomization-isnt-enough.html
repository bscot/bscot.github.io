Why Randomization Isn't Enough

A practical guide to quasi-experimental methods for measuring advertising lift when holdout groups are unavailable or impractical.

The randomized controlled trial is the gold standard for causal inference for good reason: it eliminates confounding by construction. Assign units to treatment and control at random, and any difference in outcomes can be attributed to treatment. The logic is clean. The execution, in advertising, often isn't. Platforms don't always support holdouts. Business constraints preclude them. Or the cost of the experiment — in foregone revenue, in time, in organizational friction — exceeds its expected value. Quasi-experimental methods fill this gap.

The Problem with Observational Comparisons

The naive alternative to randomization is to compare treated and untreated users directly: users who saw the ad converted at rate X; users who didn't converted at rate Y; the difference is the effect. This fails because treatment assignment is correlated with the outcome through paths that have nothing to do with the ad. Users who are served an ad are different, in many ways, from users who aren't — they tend to be more engaged, more recently active, more likely to convert regardless. Any comparison that doesn't account for these differences attributes to the ad what belongs to the user.

The statistical name for this is confounding. The practical name for it is wasted budget and misallocated attention. Observational comparisons without adjustment for confounders systematically overestimate ad effectiveness, which is why channels that target high-intent users always look good in last-touch attribution models.

Difference-in-Differences

DiD exploits the timing of treatment rather than its assignment. If some units receive a campaign and others don't, and if the two groups were trending similarly before the campaign began, then the post-minus-pre difference in the treated group minus the same difference in the control group is a credible estimate of the treatment effect. The identifying assumption — parallel trends — says that in the absence of treatment, the two groups would have continued along the same trajectory. This can never be verified directly, but it can be tested in pre-treatment data and made more plausible by covariate matching.

DiD is most useful when there is a clear treatment onset and a plausible comparison group — a geographic market, a platform, a customer segment that was not exposed. It is least useful when treatment and control groups are selected in ways that make parallel trends implausible, which in advertising is common.

Synthetic Controls

When no natural control group exists, synthetic controls construct one. A synthetic control is a weighted average of untreated units chosen to reproduce the pre-treatment trajectory of the treated unit as closely as possible. The weights are chosen by optimization; the fit is visible in a plot; and the post-treatment divergence between the treated unit and its synthetic counterpart estimates the effect of treatment.

The method is transparent in a way that regression-based approaches are not. The donor pool is explicit. The weights are interpretable. And because the entire pre-treatment fit is visible, it is easy to see whether the synthetic control is credible before looking at the post-treatment period. This separation of the design stage from the analysis stage is good scientific practice regardless of the method.

What to Watch For

Quasi-experiments are not experiments. The identifying assumptions — parallel trends, exclusion restrictions, continuity at a threshold — are always contestable and sometimes wrong. The right response is not to pretend they hold but to test them where possible, to present placebo analyses that would detect violations, and to communicate the assumptions and their plausibility alongside the estimates. An estimate with explicit, testable assumptions is more useful than one that appears assumption-free because its assumptions are hidden.

The deeper point is that no method for causal inference from observational data is assumption-free. Randomization is special because its identifying assumption — that assignment is independent of potential outcomes — is guaranteed by design rather than argued from data. Every other method substitutes a different assumption, one that has to be earned through design, argument, and evidence rather than bestowed by the experimental protocol.
