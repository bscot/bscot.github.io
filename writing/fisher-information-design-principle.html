Fisher Information as a Design Principle

How the geometry of statistical models — curvature, distance, and information flow — can guide the design of experiments and the choice of estimators.

The Fisher information matrix encodes how much a dataset, on average, tells you about the parameters of a statistical model. It is a local measure of curvature in the likelihood surface — how sharply the data distinguishes nearby parameter values. Most introductions present it as a technical device for computing standard errors. It is more interesting than that. Understood as a geometric object, it provides a set of design principles that are useful across experimental design, model selection, and machine learning.

What the Fisher Information Measures

For a model with parameters θ, the Fisher information is the expected value of the squared score — the gradient of the log-likelihood. Intuitively, it measures how much the likelihood changes as you vary the parameters: high information means the likelihood is sharply peaked and the data is constraining; low information means the likelihood is flat and the data is relatively uninformative. The matrix generalizes this to multiple parameters, capturing both the information about each parameter and the correlations between them.

The Cramér-Rao bound is the basic result: the variance of any unbiased estimator is bounded below by the inverse of the Fisher information. More information means tighter estimates, and no unbiased procedure can do better than this bound. This gives a natural objective for experimental design: choose observations that maximize the information about the parameters you care about.

Information Geometry

Viewing the space of probability distributions as a Riemannian manifold, with the Fisher information as the metric tensor, gives a natural notion of distance between statistical models. Two distributions are close in this geometry if it is hard to distinguish them from data; they are far apart if even a small sample makes them easily separable. This is a more operationally meaningful notion of distance than, say, Euclidean distance on the parameter space, which depends on arbitrary choices of parameterization.

The most direct application is the natural gradient, introduced by Amari. Ordinary gradient descent follows the steepest direction in Euclidean parameter space. Natural gradient descent follows the steepest direction in information space — it accounts for the curvature of the model and takes steps that are invariant to reparameterization. For models where the Fisher information can be computed or approximated efficiently, natural gradient methods converge faster and are better behaved near saddle points.

Designing Experiments with Information

An optimal experiment is one that places observations where the Fisher information is highest — where small changes in parameters produce the largest changes in the likelihood. For linear models, this leads to D-optimal design, which maximizes the determinant of the information matrix and thereby minimizes the volume of the confidence ellipsoid for the parameters. For nonlinear models, the optimal design depends on the current parameter estimates, leading to adaptive or sequential designs that update as data arrives.

In practice, optimal design often produces designs that concentrate observations at the extremes of the design space, which can be fragile to model misspecification. A more robust approach is to design for a minimax criterion — minimizing the worst-case information loss over a neighborhood of plausible models. This reflects the same tension that appears throughout statistics between efficiency under the assumed model and robustness to departures from it.

Connections to Bayesian Methods

The Fisher information is a frequentist object, but it connects naturally to Bayesian experimental design through the expected information gain — the KL divergence between posterior and prior, averaged over possible datasets. Maximizing this quantity is equivalent to maximizing the expected reduction in uncertainty and reduces to Fisher-based criteria in the large-sample limit, where the posterior is approximately Gaussian with covariance equal to the inverse Fisher information.

This connection is practically useful because it provides a coherent framework for sequential experimental design: run an experiment, update the posterior, choose the next observation to maximize expected information gain, repeat. The Bayesian framing makes it natural to incorporate prior knowledge and to reason about the value of information explicitly — how much is the next experiment worth, given what you already know?
