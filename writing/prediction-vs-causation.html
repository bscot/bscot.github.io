The Difference Between Prediction and Causation

On why high model accuracy is neither necessary nor sufficient for good decisions, and what causal thinking offers that predictive modeling cannot.

A predictive model answers one question: given everything I observe, what is the most likely outcome? A causal model answers a different question: if I intervene — if I change something — what will happen? These are not the same question. Confusing them leads to systematically bad decisions, and the confusion is easy to make because good predictive models often feel like causal understanding.

The Accuracy Trap

High accuracy can coexist with complete causal misunderstanding. A model trained to predict conversion rates from historical ad exposure data will likely perform well on held-out data — and will likely mislead you about the effect of increasing ad spend. The model has learned correlations that reflect who tends to buy; it has not learned what causes purchases. When you act on its predictions, you change the distribution it was trained on.

This is not a failure of the model. The model is doing exactly what it was asked to do. The failure is in the question. Predictive accuracy is the right objective for forecasting; it is the wrong objective for decision-making. A decision is an intervention. Interventions require causal, not predictive, models.

Observational vs. Interventional Distributions

The mathematical distinction is between P(Y | X) and P(Y | do(X)). The first is the conditional distribution of outcomes given that you observe X. The second is the distribution after you set X by intervention — after you reach in and fix its value regardless of whatever would naturally have produced it. These two distributions are equal only when X is unconfounded: when there is no common cause of X and Y that you haven't conditioned on. In most real-world settings, this condition fails.

Pearl's do-calculus gives a set of rules for moving between these distributions when the causal structure is known. The structure is encoded in a directed acyclic graph — a set of assumptions about what causes what. These assumptions can be wrong. But making them explicit means they can be tested, debated, and updated. Predictive models encode no assumptions about mechanism. This makes them easier to build and harder to reason about when the goal is action.

What Causal Models Require

Causal identification typically requires one of three things: an experiment, a credible quasi-experiment, or strong structural assumptions backed by domain knowledge. Experiments are the most reliable; they eliminate confounding by construction. Quasi-experiments exploit natural variation — timing discontinuities, geographic boundaries, instrument variables — to approximate the conditions of an experiment. Structural assumptions, used carefully, can identify causal effects from observational data alone, but they are the most fragile of the three.

The honest question to ask of any causal claim is: what is the source of identification? Where does the causal signal come from? If the answer is "the correlation in the data," the claim is not causal. If the answer is "a randomized holdout" or "a regression discontinuity" or "a structural model with tested assumptions," the claim has a foundation worth examining.

Practical Implications

The practical upshot is that causal reasoning changes what you measure, not just how you model it. You need variation that is, in some defensible sense, independent of the confounders you're worried about. This is a constraint on data collection, not just data analysis — which is why causal thinking should inform experimental design before a single observation is made.

The good news is that partial identification is often enough. You don't need to identify every causal effect in a system, only the one you are going to act on. A narrow, well-identified causal estimate is worth more for decision-making than a broad, accurate predictive model — even if the predictive model explains far more variance.
